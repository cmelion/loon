# Envelope debris and emissions: how much, where it lands, what each mitigation does to it, and the launch comparison both ways

*Research note, 2026-10-17. Answers one question from the author: the envelope litter is significant and, unlike a satellite burning up or a launch plume, it is a visible artefact; how big is it, how does it compare, and what do the mitigations do to the number? Builds on `results/cost_comparison.md`, `results/envelope_cost.md`, `results/mitigation_research.md` (W21, W47) and `results/deployable_now.md`. Every figure is an estimate unless a source is named; the arithmetic is in the text so it can be redone.*

## What comes down, per balloon

A superpressure envelope is polyethylene film. The three vehicles in this repository shed these amounts per flight:

| Vehicle | Envelope mass | Film gauge | Film area if laid flat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light, 40 kg payload (`results/envelope_cost.md` §4) | 17 kg | 25–30 µm | about 665 m², a 26 m square |
| Loon-class, the simulator's balloon | 68.5 kg | about 59 µm | about 1,250 m² |
| Class M, 90–100 kg payload (`vehicle.py` HEAVY, assumption) | 60 kg | about 59 µm | about 1,100 m² |

The area is the number a bystander meets: a field with a 26 m square of film in it, or a hillside with the sheet draped over the trees, plus tendons, fittings and the flight computer if nothing recovered it. The mass is the number permits are written against. Both are small per flight and large per fleet, which is why the architecture decides this question and the material does not.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The land fraction of 66 % and the pickup rate are from `analyze_envelopes.py` and `results/envelope_cost.md` (f); the box and pickup columns are assumptions to be replaced by the release-timing and recovery work in the mitigation plan, and the retrieval rate at sea has to be demonstrated, not assumed, before the sea is chosen. The 300,000-flight figure is the article's; `results/cost_comparison.md` carries 27,500 t a year for the baseline fleet on today's envelope with a larger launch count, and the difference is the fleet size each note assumed, not the per-flight mass.

Two things follow. First, the debris problem is a property of the planning case, not of balloons: short winter flights on a light vehicle at hundreds of launches a day put thousands of tonnes on the ground, and no recovery scheme gets that below several hundred. Second, the readiness architecture the mitigation review chose, a thin standing layer on long flights with surge from a few sites, is two orders of magnitude smaller before any mitigation, and a few tonnes a year after it, which is a quantity a country already deals with in weather balloons: Europe's meteorological services release roughly a hundred radiosondes a day whose latex and payloads are not recovered.

## How it compares

The comparison the author asked for is with things the public does and does not see.

| Source | Where it ends up | Material | Amount a year | Visible? | Recoverable? | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balloon envelopes, planning case | ground and sea over Europe | polyethylene film, tendons, fittings | 5,100 t down, 700–2,700 t unrecovered | yes, a sheet in a field | partly, by pickup | this note |
| Balloon envelopes, readiness layer | ground, mostly in drop zones | same | 26 t down, 4–14 t unrecovered | yes | mostly | this note |
| Fibre-optic drone cable, Ukraine | fields, forests, towns along the front | glass fibre with acrylate coating | about 100,000 drones a month with spools over 10 km, so 1–2 million km a month; the Conflict and Environment Observatory put Russia's need at 6 million km in 2025; at about 70 g a kilometre that is roughly 400 t a year for one side, 500–1,000 t for both (my mass estimate from fibre geometry; nobody has weighed it) | yes, and it tangles machinery, animals and deminers | no, in practice | Ukrainska Pravda, May 2026; CEOBS via NPR, August 2025; Meduza, July 2026 |
| Starlink satellites re-entering | upper atmosphere, 60–80 km, as metal oxide particles | aluminium and alumina, with lithium, copper and others | 1–4 satellites a day in 2025–26 at 300–800 kg each is 100–1,200 t of satellite mass a year; alumina alone was 17 t in 2022 across all re-entries and is projected at 360 t a year at full megaconstellation build (Ferreira et al., GRL 2024) | no | no | USC study via earth.com; McDowell's counts via secondary reports |
| Launches to keep a constellation up | stratosphere and above | CO₂, water, black carbon, alumina | a Falcon 9 launch emits roughly 200–390 t of CO₂ and tonnes of soot into the stratosphere; at about a hundred launches a year that is 20,000–40,000 t of CO₂ and hundreds of tonnes of black carbon, where it persists two to three years and nothing else deposits it | no | no | Interesting Engineering on Ross's work; The Space Review; one vendor figure for soot, unverified |

The reading. Per tonne, the envelope is the least harmful of the four: inert polyethylene on the ground, which can be picked up, against glass that cannot, alumina that chemistry models now link to stratospheric circulation, and soot at an altitude where it is the only soot. Per photograph, the envelope is the most harmful of the four, because it is the only one a person can stand next to, and an envelope lost at sea is the one version of it with no photograph and no finder, which is why the sea rule above stands or falls on the retrieval rate. The planning case would put more visible plastic on European ground each year than the drone war puts fibre on Ukrainian ground, and that comparison, not the per-tonne one, is the one the public would make. The readiness layer puts down less than one per cent of that. Both halves are true and the note that argues for the layer should say both.

## What each mitigation does to the number

In the order the mitigation review ranked them, with the effect on the readiness layer's 26 t a year and the planning case's 5,100 t:

1. **Fly fewer, longer flights.** Already the decision: readiness instead of the planning case, class M on 100-day flights instead of the light vehicle on two-day winter crossings. This is the ÷200 in the table, and no other line comes close. The light vehicle's film is thinner, but it flies fifty times as often per balloon-day.
2. **Time the end of flight for a recovery box at sea, with ships waiting; land only where no box is downwind.** The radio leaves on the glider for a recovery site; the envelope is not steered. What the release rule can do is choose the hour, and the author's preference is that the hour puts the envelope in a designated sea box where a vessel retrieves it: no landowner, no livestock, no photograph. The forecast that decides when the glider goes can prefer an hour whose envelope landing, a median 26 km downwind of the cut-down in `analyze_envelopes.py`, falls inside a box, and the barometric vent element above has finished the gas by then. This is the sensitive part of the whole system, because the failure mode inverts: an envelope that misses the box and is not found is marine plastic with no finder, which is a worse story than the one in the field. So the sea is only the right answer where the retrieval rate is demonstrated: polyethylene floats, a beacon and a sea-anchor drogue keep the envelope findable for days, the boxes sit on the exit corridors the winter tracks actually use (the Atlantic approaches in from the west, the Black Sea and the eastern Mediterranean out), and a vessel per box is in the standing cost. For the readiness layer that is a few hundred envelopes a year across three or four boxes, one or two vessels' work; for the planning case it is eight hundred envelopes a day, which is a fleet, and that is W47's uncosted line. Modelled here as 90 % landing in a box and 90 % of those retrieved: 26 t becomes 5 t a year; 5,100 t becomes 970 t. Where no box is downwind, the rule falls back to a land landing and the pickup bounty below.
3. **Pay for pickup.** `cost_model.py` already charges €100–1,500 an envelope for pickup; `results/envelope_cost.md` finds the resin worth about €4 and the scheme worth having for litter, not money. A beacon on the envelope and a bounty raise the land pickup rate; 70 % to 90 % is the assumption used above.
4. **Ships.** W47 costed nothing for them. Under the rule above they are not a mitigation for accidental sea landings but the planned retrieval at the end of every flight whose hour put the envelope in a box, so they move from a contingency to the standing cost: one or two vessels for the readiness layer, a fleet for the planning case. The vessel also carries the hydrogen electrolyser the article proposed for ships, so the same hull launches and retrieves.
5. **Material.** No biodegradable film holds 1,000 Pa for weeks at −70 °C; bio-based polyethylene is still polyethylene on the ground; recycling credit is negligible (W21, `results/envelope_cost.md`). Material changes the chemistry of what is left, not the quantity, and belongs after the first four.
6. **Burning at altitude.** Rejected: `burn_sim.py` found a median 90 % consumed, shreds over a 200 km strip, 215 kg of CO₂ a balloon, and a permitting problem worse than litter.
7. **Permit against the residual.** Under MARPOL Annex V for the sea and the single-use plastics regime on land, a quantified loss rate with a recovery plan is a permit application; an unquantified one is a campaign against the project. The readiness layer's residual of 4–14 t a year across a continent is a quantity to put in the application; the planning case's 700–2,700 t is not.

## Emissions, both ways

The comparison above counts the other side's emissions and not ours. The layer has two of its own: the hydrogen that lifts every balloon, and the polyethylene it is made of. Both scale with flights, so the same architecture decision that governs the litter governs these.

Inputs, all assumptions to be replaced by measurement: electrolysis at 52 kWh per kg of hydrogen including compression; grid electricity at 0.20–0.25 t CO₂ per MWh, the EU band for 2025, or about zero on dedicated renewables; polyethylene film at 1.8–2.5 t CO₂e per tonne, cradle to gate; hydrogen per flight 4.6 kg for the light vehicle (`vehicle.py`), 14 kg for the Loon-class balloon (the article's figure), 12.6 kg for class M. `results/cost_comparison.md` carries 6,400 t of hydrogen and 338 GWh a year for its larger baseline fleet, the same order as the planning-case row here.

| Scenario | Hydrogen a year | Electricity | CO₂ from hydrogen on the grid | CO₂e from the film | Total on the grid | Total on renewable hydrogen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planning case, light vehicle, 300,000 flights | 1,400 t | 72 GWh | 14,000–18,000 t | 9,000–13,000 t | 23,000–31,000 t | 9,000–13,000 t |
| Planning case, Loon-class envelope | 4,200 t | 218 GWh | 44,000–55,000 t | 37,000–51,000 t | 81,000–106,000 t | 37,000–51,000 t |
| Readiness layer, about 430 flights | 5 t | 0.3 GWh | 56–70 t | 46–64 t | about 100–130 t | about 50–65 t |
| Standing-layer pilot, about 90 flights | 1 t | 0.06 GWh | 12–15 t | 10–14 t | about 25 t | about 12 t |

**The lift gas itself.** Hydrogen is not a greenhouse gas, but it behaves as an indirect one: it consumes the hydroxyl radicals that remove methane, adds tropospheric ozone, and in the stratosphere oxidises to water vapour. The reference figure is a 100-year warming potential of 11.6 ± 2.8 kg of CO₂ per kg of hydrogen from a five-model study (Sand et al., 2023), which the same group's 2025 follow-up found insensitive to the size and location of the release except at sites far from the soil sink that removes hydrogen at the surface. A balloon venting at 20 km is exactly such a site, so the high end of the range applies, and the 20-year figure is about three times higher. This layer vents its lift gas at the end of every flight, and that is a release the launch side has no counterpart to.

| Scenario | Hydrogen vented a year | CO₂e at 11.6 (8.8–14.4) per kg | Added to the grid-hydrogen total above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planning case, light vehicle | 1,400 t | 16,000 t (12,000–20,000) | 39,000–51,000 t a year in all |
| Planning case, Loon-class envelope | 4,200 t | 49,000 t (37,000–60,000) | 118,000–166,000 t a year in all |
| Readiness layer | 5 t | about 60 t | about 160–190 t a year in all |
| Standing-layer pilot | 1 t | about 12 t | about 37 t a year in all |

**Oxidise it first, on the envelope.** The author's answer, and the right one: do not vent hydrogen, convert it to water as it leaves. The radio has already gone on the glider, the envelope is not aimed anywhere, so the device has to ride the envelope: cheap against a €1,070 envelope, a kilogram or two against 17, and without power to speak of. That is a catalytic vent element: the apex valve exhausts through a platinum-on-ceramic gauze behind a flame arrestor, on a short standoff above the film, flameless, driven by the envelope's own venting as it collapses and descends, and lost with the envelope. The catalyst metal is grams, the same as a car's converter, so €30–150 of platinum in a €100–300 part. Three facts size it:

- *Heat.* Hydrogen gives 33 kWh a kilogram, so the light vehicle's 4.6 kg is 150 kWh and class M's 12.6 kg is 420 kWh, all of it released at the gauze. Over a one-hour descent that is 150 and 420 kW; over six hours, 25 and 70 kW. A glowing gauze sheds on the order of 60 kW per square metre, so the element is 0.4–2.5 m² for a one-hour descent and a fraction of that if the valve lets the envelope down over several hours. The envelope's own descent sets the vent rate, and a barometric valve that opens wider as the air thickens makes the descent slow high up and faster low down, which is also what the chemistry wants.
- *Air.* Each kilogram of hydrogen needs about 35 kg of air for its oxygen, 90 m³ at 400 hPa and 600 m³ at 55 hPa. At float there is not enough; below the tropopause there is. The same barometric valve therefore vents little in the stratosphere, where water would linger for years, and most in the troposphere, where it rains out in days and the gauze runs well. Natural draft from the hot plume and the airflow of descent supply it; no fan.
- *Safety.* The hazard is not the gas in the envelope, which has no oxygen to burn with, but a tear that mixes air into it with a hot element nearby: `burn_sim.py`'s case, rejected for the shreds it scatters. The arrestor, the standoff and a flameless catalyst are the answer, and the chamber test at 55–400 hPa with a film sample under the element is the first thing to buy.

The class M fuel cell still helps on its own flights: the gas it burns through the last nights never reaches the vent, and the M4's runs show 10–17 % consumed that way. Whatever the gauze misses, leakage and the high-altitude fraction, is counted as hydrogen in the table; the rest goes in as water, at roughly a tenth of the warming if made high and nil if made low. This is a register item in its own right and is added as W51.

Two mitigations exist and one does not. A fuel cell that burns part of the lift gas through the night (the class M winter mode, 10–17 % of the gas per flight in the M4's runs) turns that fraction into water instead of venting it, which still lands in the stratosphere but as a small amount of water vapour rather than hydrogen. Longer flights vent less per balloon-day, which is the readiness architecture again. Helium avoids the chemistry entirely but is scarce, four to five times the price, and cannot feed a fuel cell, which is why the register (W22) keeps hydrogen. Electrolyser and transfer leakage, 1–10 % of throughput in the literature, is a further few per cent on top of the vented figure and is not in the table.

Not counted on our side: recovery vehicles and ships, launch-site construction, the radios and their refurbishment, and the electricity of the ground network. They are small per flight and real per fleet, and the scenario ledger (rerun 9) is where they belong.

The launch side. A Falcon 9 launch emits roughly 200–390 t of CO₂, and the Starlink programme flies on the order of 100–140 of them a year, so 20,000–55,000 t of CO₂ a year for the whole constellation's replenishment, plus tonnes of black carbon per launch into the stratosphere and the alumina from re-entries above. Europe's share of that constellation is 3–5 % of its satellite-time (`results/deployable_now.md`), which puts Europe's pro-rata share of Starlink's launch CO₂ at about 600–2,700 t a year. Satellite manufacturing and the ground network are not in that figure either, and no public number exists for them per satellite.

The reading, scenario by scenario. The planning case on grid hydrogen emits 23,000–31,000 t of CO₂e a year on the light vehicle before the vented gas and 39,000–51,000 t with it, ten to forty times Europe's pro-rata share of Starlink's launches and the same order as the whole constellation's launch programme; on today's envelope it is three to four times that. On renewable hydrogen, which is what the article's Quebec and British Columbia hydro and the on-site electrolysis in W22 mean, the electricity term falls away but the vented gas and the film remain, 25,000–33,000 t, still well above Starlink's European share. The readiness layer emits 160–190 t a year on the grid, including its vented gas, and about 110 t on renewables, which is the annual footprint of a few dozen European households and below the measurement noise of any national inventory. What the launch side has and the balloon side does not is the stratospheric particulates: black carbon at 20 km and above, where rockets are the only source, and alumina from re-entries, which 2026 modelling links to changes in polar circulation; neither has a balloon counterpart, and per tonne they matter more than CO₂. What the balloon side has and the launch side does not is the litter above. The honest sentence is that at the planning case's scale the layer is the larger emitter of CO₂ and the only visible polluter; at the readiness scale it is negligible on both counts, and the comparison that matters is particulates against plastic, not tonnes of CO₂.

**The denominator.** None of the rows above is a like-for-like comparison, and the note should not pretend they are. A constellation's launches and re-entries are a constant: they happen whether or not any European needs service that day, and Europe's pro-rata share is an accounting convention, not a dial anyone can turn. The layer's emissions are a dial. They scale with flights, flights scale with the service being flown, and the service is targeted: balloons go where and when they are needed and nowhere else, which is the property the whole case rests on (`results/target_population.md`). The planning case is what the dial reads at a permanent 95 % blanket; the readiness layer is what it reads when the layer is tuned to the demand that exists, a thin standing presence and surge on call. So the fair comparison is per unit of service delivered, emissions per protected site-day or per emergency session, with the constellation's constant divided by what Europe draws from it and the layer's dial read at the service level actually chosen. That figure needs the availability model (rerun 6) and the scenario ledger (rerun 9) and is not computed here. Until it is, the table above should be read as bounds: at full blanket the layer out-emits Starlink's European share, tuned to need it is negligible, and the tuning is the point.

## What this changes in the other notes

`results/deployable_now.md`'s approvals table has an environmental row that cites W21 and W47; it should carry the readiness layer's residual (4–14 t a year, estimate) so the permitting conversation starts from a number. The article's unsolved-risks bullet gives the planning case's 5,000 t and is correct for the fleet it describes; the next installment should say that the architecture the study now proposes puts down about 1 % of that, and show the comparison table. The weakness register's W21 keeps its severity for the planning case and gains the readiness figure. The emissions table belongs beside the cost table in the next installment, with the hydrogen source stated, because on grid hydrogen the planning case is a larger CO₂ emitter than Starlink's European share and that is a sentence a critic will write first.
