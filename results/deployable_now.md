# What can be deployed now, and the upgrade path

*Research note, 2026-10-17. Builds on `results/mitigation_research.md`, `results/radio_and_backhaul.md`, `results/target_population.md` and `results/cost_funding_vs_starlink.md`. Every cost is an estimate unless a source is named. Vendor prices for stratospheric flight services are not public; where a figure is a placeholder for a quote it says so. Reviewed the same day by three external models and a second Claude session; the record is at the end.*

## What "now" means

Two different things can be bought in the next year.

**The surge service itself.** For a region whose towers fail for days, the bundle priced in the mitigation addendum is on sale: satellite terminals pre-positioned at hospitals, stations and vehicles; pooled deployable cells with satellite backhaul; a direct-to-cell standby contract through a national operator; GOVSATCOM capacity for institutions, with IRIS² from 2029. It covers the winter voice-and-text case at roughly €0.5–1.3 per EU resident a year and is contractable in 2026–27 from catalogue items. It should be bought first. The balloon layer has to justify itself by the three things the bundle leaves out: a Mbps-class service to ordinary phones, control that does not run through a foreign operator's contract, and capacity added at the incident rather than drawn from a shared regional pool.

**A balloon pilot, not a balloon service.** One vehicle class with multi-month flight heritage, a radio that exists as a ground product, one cooperative state, and instruments to answer the questions the earlier panels left open. The dates need care. A Mediterranean summer campaign fits inside twelve months. A northern winter campaign does not: permissions filed in the first quarter of 2027 do not make January 2027, so the first honest winter window is 2027–28, thirteen to sixteen months out. The winter window is the one that decides the architecture.

## Configuration A: the pilot

**Vehicle.** Aerostar Thunderhead, contracted as a flight service. It is the only steerable stratospheric balloon I can find with commercial multi-month flights and a published payload in the tens of kilograms: operating altitudes 17–23 km, typical missions near 100 days, a 336-day record flight, navigation by machine-learned wind models over a wider altitude band than this repository's simulator uses. Three qualifications. The 336-day flight is duration heritage, not evidence of useful dwell over northern Europe with this payload and this night load; that is what the pilot measures. Aerostar's two public payload figures, 57 kg and 91 kg, are 125 lb and 200 lb and almost certainly two configurations; until Aerostar names the configuration that flies in winter, with an interface document giving customer mass after termination and recovery hardware, continuous and night bus power, and altitude band, the planning allowance for the whole integrated payload is 40 kg. And Aerostar is a US company, which is acceptable for a pilot and not for the sovereign layer, so the upgrade path includes a European vehicle. No alternative meets all four tests of steerable, 40 kg class, winter heritage and purchasable in 2027. Zephyr carries about 5 kg and reaches commercial service in 2027–28. Sceye is an airship, pre-commercial in Japan, with no published payload. Urban Sky and Near Space Labs fly small imaging balloons. CNES and SSC operate excellent scientific balloons, some of them superpressure, but as campaigns, unsteered, not as a service. World View's Stratollite is the nearest comparator on paper and should get a request for quotation alongside Aerostar.

**Price.** Unknown. No per-flight or per-day tariff is public, and defence programme totals do not convert into one. The quotation is the first number to obtain this quarter. The budget below uses €0.5–2 M per multi-week flight as a placeholder so that the arithmetic can be shown; it is not a pricing signal.

**Payload, 40 kg integrated.** The radio is a private-network LTE or NR small cell from a compact product range (Baicells, Nokia or an SDR base station; Airspan is a weak bet after its restructuring), 2–5 kg and 50–150 W as a ground unit. The airborne subsystem is not that: filters, timing, power conversion, thermal control at 50–100 hPa and −60 °C, radome, cabling and mounts make a 10–25 kg allowance safer before antennas and backhaul. Two to four sector antennas of about 1 m on a stabilised mount, specified by gain and beamwidth rather than diameter. Operation in a host operator's band under a national test licence with 10–20 MHz. Backhaul by an E-band link to a ground gateway at a design range of 30–50 km, which keeps the elevation above about 20°; 100 km from 20 km altitude is an elevation of 11° through the whole rain layer and is a measurement objective, not the plan. A Ka-band satellite terminal as the all-weather fallback, costed in mass and power and acknowledged as the satellite dependence it is. Night power is the project: 150 W through the 17-hour December night of a 60°N campaign is 2.55 kWh (about 15 hours at 50°N) before heaters and backhaul, and the vehicle's bus has to be shown to carry it. The desk budget run on 17 October 2026 (`winter_power.py`, 50°N, 60 hPa; `results/reruns_2026-10-17.md` §3) puts a 300 W night on the light vehicle at 4.5 kWh in December, a 37.5 kg battery at 150 Wh/kg that overruns the 40 kg payload; a larger array does not help, the 15-hour night does. Those are estimates from a model without cloud or frost; the 150 W figure here is what they leave room for. Recovery by parachute or guided parafoil into a pre-cleared drop zone, following scientific-balloon practice, with a national safety case; a cleared zone does not guarantee the vehicle reaches it, and third-party liability has to be insured. A flight-termination system per ICAO Annex 2. Planning capacity 20 Mbps per balloon, ceiling 100 Mbps, the band between Loon's Kenya figures and the regulator-measured Saudi single-cell test in `results/radio_and_backhaul.md`. No glider, no laser, no fuel cell in this phase.

**Ground.** One gateway site with the E-band head and fibre; a deployable core (Athonet, Druid or Open5GS class) with priority classes; 112 routed through the host operator; a handset matrix of 20–50 unmodified phones across chipsets. Ordinary SIMs attaching is an operator agreement, not a feature: Release 17 disaster roaming and MOCN both need the host operator's authentication, lawful-intercept and emergency-call arrangements, and a test licence does not authorise public service. After political will, the host operator's spectrum and these agreements are the long pole.

**Where and when.** Two campaigns in one or two states. A summer campaign in 2027 in a Mediterranean state for the fire-watch and dwell case. A winter campaign in 2027–28 at 55–62°N, in Scotland or southern Sweden; Esrange is at 68°N, a harsher case with different winds, and is an option rather than the default. Permissions are national. Annex 2 sets the rules for unmanned free balloons; the authorisation, notification, termination, overflight and recovery conditions come from the state's aviation, spectrum, maritime and local authorities. Six to twelve months is the planning assumption, with written pre-application answers from the regulators before contracts are signed.

**What it measures.** Indoor and edge uplink closure at 14–20° elevation, which no demonstration has published; handset attach, timing, handover and 112 behaviour on a cell moving at 10–70 km/h; night power through real 16-hour nights; E-band gateway availability through winter weather; the vehicle's actual dwell against the simulator's, which is the first real test of weakness W1 and of Aerostar's wind models at this latitude; time from call to coverage in one unannounced drill; and recovery accuracy to a drop zone.

**Cost.** With the placeholder flight price, 3–6 flights are €1.5–12 M. Payload integration, thermal-vacuum qualification and three payload sets €1–2 M; gateway, core and handsets €0.3–0.8 M; regulatory, legal, insurance and drop-zone arrangements €0.3–0.8 M; a team of eight to twelve for eighteen months €2–4 M. That is €5–20 M before contingency; with the 30–50 % contingency a programme without a vehicle quote deserves, **€10–20 M with the overrun on the upside**. Fundable from one national civil-protection or research budget. A pilot with no second-year line is a press release, so the line for Configuration B belongs in the same decision.

## Configuration B: the standing-layer pilot, 2028–29

After a winter campaign, the same vehicle and payload scale to a small standing layer: 6–12 balloons aloft over one state or a pair of neighbours, from two launch sites, recovered by parachute. With flights near 100 days that is 22–44 launches a year plus losses, not launches every day. The launch-rate rehearsal is separate: a two-week surge exercise once a year at 3–6 launches a day, 40–80 flights, with the vehicles budgeted as consumed. Two or three balloons carry an optical terminal, a named and weighed unit, to measure acquisition, pointing and link rate between drifting platforms; that is a terminal experiment, not a mesh, and the 50–100-balloon mesh floor from the earlier note stands. Cost at the placeholder flight price: 60–120 flights a year, €30–120 M a year, plus sites and crews. That range is the clearest argument for getting a vehicle price and for the European vehicle. Capacity is still 20–100 Mbps a balloon. The product is responder connectivity every day and voice-and-text for the public in drills, and it should be described as that.

## What is useful on day one

- **A responder channel the state runs.** One balloon over a flooded valley or a fire line gives several hundred responders voice, push-to-talk and location on ordinary phones, with priority classes the host sets and no clause in a foreign contract deciding who is served. Loon delivered that in Peru and Puerto Rico. Civil-protection agencies have bought it before; whether they buy it from a balloon is the pilot's commercial question.
- **Measured numbers where there are none.** Uplink closure, handset behaviour and night power at a mid-latitude winter have not been published by anyone. They decide whether this vehicle and payload class belongs in the resilience mix; they do not decide the question for aircraft HAPS, tethered systems or other seasons.
- **A drill that includes the air.** A surge drill that launches a real balloon into a real exercise rehearses the launch, airspace and recovery loops that the catalogue bundle never exercises. Those loops are the slow part of readiness.

## Where it scales, and what that does to the cost case

**Production.** The vehicle is an envelope and a flight computer; the payload is a radio and antennas. At volume the envelope and the avionics are not the expensive items. The expensive items are operations, launch crews, recovery, gateways and airspace work, which are mostly fixed per site and per day, so the cost of a balloon-day falls as flights last longer and sites launch more often. The step from class L to class M roughly doubles vehicle mass and brings a phased array, kilowatt-class power, a fuel cell and parafoil recovery; the capacity per flight rises somewhere between 5× and 75× depending on which ends of the two ranges are compared. A European vehicle built in the hundreds is the second lever against a single US supplier's defence pricing. Neither lever has a number yet; phase 2 exists to get them.

**Participation.** A standing layer sized for one event costs the same whether one state or twenty can call it, but only for that entitlement. Each added state brings its own permissions, gateway heads, drop zones and exercises, which it funds, and adds little to the flight bill until it surges; two simultaneous events need two reserves. So the cost per contributing resident falls with participation, which is the rescEU model, while the cost per protected resident per event-day does not. Both should be reported. A layer that costs one state €3–24 per resident a year costs the Union well under €1 when pooled; the bundle's costs scale the other way for a public buyer, since every state draws on the same cells and the price is set abroad.

**Surge.** Surge capacity scales with launch sites and crews, which cost the same per day whether or not they are used. The planning case's €6.3 B a year is that cost on a hemispheric loop with gliders; the mitigation plan shrinks it by dwell and recovery, not by cheaper hardware.

Phase 4 against the bundle is the comparison that matters, and it cannot be made yet: phase 4 is unmodelled and six years away, the bundle is on sale, and a finance ministry buys what exists. The honest statement is narrower. If phase 3 measures capacity near the top of its range, the layer adds capacity at the incident, control in the host state and a public data product, for a cost in the bundle's band. If it measures near the bottom, the advantage is responders and control alone, and the recommendation is phase 2 as a permanent small layer, not phase 4. Where terminals and deployable cells are still needed, their cost is additive, not replaced.

**The benefit side.** It should not be a blackout's GDP loss. The 28 April 2025 Iberian blackout cut power for most of a day; the Spanish government later put the economic effect near €0.2 B, CaixaBank under €0.4 B, the employers' federation CEOE €1.6 B and RBC €2.25–4.5 B, and none of those is a communications figure. They bound a one-day national outage; they do not price a balloon, and balloons do not restore power. The benefit is the communications-specific harm the layer avoids: emergency calls completed, dispatch delays avoided, hospital and utility coordination kept, the option value of capacity when satellite cells congest, and a sovereignty premium that should be stated as political rather than priced. Those need incident probabilities and the fraction of harm the layer can actually prevent, which `results/target_population.md` begins and this note does not finish.

## What a comparable constellation costs

**There is no regional LEO constellation.** A satellite in a Starlink-class shell spends a small fraction of its time within reach of the Union, so regional capacity from low orbit is bought as a share of a global system. Inclined geosynchronous and highly elliptical architectures can be regional, but they are not Starlink-class in latency or capacity. The share can be computed. A circular orbit at 53° inclination with uniformly distributed phases has a sub-satellite point over EU territory 2.4 % of the time; within 500 km of EU territory, roughly the serving footprint at a 25° elevation mask, 3.9 %; within 900 km, 5.1 % (Monte Carlo over EU bounding boxes totalling 8.6 M km² including coastal sea; land alone is 4.2 M km², so the first figure is an upper bound for subpoint occupancy). A 53° shell also cannot serve north of about 61–62° at that mask, which matters for Sweden and Finland and is why the higher-inclination shells carry the far north. Beam steering and frequency reuse mean sellable capacity is not strictly proportional to that time, so the defensible statement is: 2–5 % of a 53° shell's satellite-time is within reach of the Union, under the metric stated.

The last row is the competitor a civil-protection reader will think of first. A Starlink-class system cannot be built at SpaceX's price by anyone who does not own its launcher and its satellite factory. SpaceX flies on the order of a hundred launches a year and builds satellites by the dozen a week; Ariane 6's planned cadence is about a dozen a year and Europe has no factory at that rate. That is why Europe's actual answer is a 350-satellite system for institutions, and why IRIS² is the honest sovereign comparator: averaged, it is in the same cost band as the balloon readiness layer and above it, it arrives in 2029, and it does not serve phones.

## The competition for the orbit, and Kessler syndrome

**What the ITU does and does not do.** The ITU coordinates spectrum; it does not allocate orbital shells. An earlier filing gives coordination priority and interference rights in a band, not ownership of an altitude. Under WRC-19 Resolution 35 a non-geostationary filing keeps its priority only if the system is deployed on schedule: a tenth of the filed satellites within two years of the end of the regulatory period, half within five, all within seven. So the price of a large filing is a launch cadence, which is the thing Europe lacks. Amazon's missed FCC deployment deadline, a separate national obligation, shows how hard the cadence is to achieve even for the second-largest buyer of launches, for reasons that include manufacturing readiness and launch availability as well as policy.

**Who has filed.** About 12,000–13,000 satellites were active in 2026 by the public trackers, Starlink more than half. Starlink's FCC authorisations are near 12,000 across two generations with applications for tens of thousands more. China's Guowang plans about 13,000 and Qianfan about 15,000; both had launched low hundreds by mid-2026, and a further Chinese filing near 200,000 exists on paper. Amazon's licence is 3,236 with filings for about 8,000. Telesat, Hanwha, Globalstar, Logos and others have filed for tens of thousands. Forecasters put 30,000–60,000 active satellites by 2030; that is a scenario band, not a count. Launch counts should be taken from Jonathan McDowell's or CelesTrak's catalogue on the day, not from this note.

**The binding constraint is coordination, not volume.** A late European entrant is not legally excluded from any shell. It coordinates in Ku and Ka band behind the first movers, sits under the power limits that protect geostationary systems, manoeuvres around the incumbents' fleets using ephemerides they share, carries higher insurance and disposal requirements, and buys launches at Ariane 6's cadence or from the incumbents. There is no agreed carrying capacity for a shell and no traffic-management regime that promises a safe one to anybody. The risk for a newcomer is not that the orbit is full but that safe operation becomes costly and politically contested before the rules exist.

**Kessler syndrome.** The debris question is the one that turns the orbit comparison from a cost argument into a resilience argument, and it belongs here plainly stated.

- The state of the environment, from ESA's Space Environment Report 2025 (data to end 2024): about 40,000 objects tracked, about 11,000 of them active payloads; more than 1.2 million objects larger than a centimetre and over 50,000 larger than ten; at 550 km the model puts debris of threatening size at the same order of magnitude as active satellites; intact objects re-enter more than three times a day; and the report's central finding is that fragmentation events now add debris faster than the atmosphere removes it, so the population would keep growing with no further launches. That last sentence is the definition of the syndrome, and ESA's own statement that active removal is needed to stop it.
- A revised stability model by Lewis and Kessler, as summarised in early 2026, finds the intact population above the unstable threshold at all altitudes from 400 to 1,000 km and above the runaway threshold at nearly all altitudes from 520 to 1,000 km. The paper should be read before that is quoted further.
- Altitude decides how long a cascade lasts. Below about 500 km the atmosphere clears debris in years; Starlink's shells at 340–570 km are chosen for that. Above 1,000 km, fragments persist for centuries. The 2007 Chinese anti-satellite test at about 860 km left more than 3,000 tracked fragments, most still in orbit; the 2009 Iridium–Cosmos collision near 790 km about 2,000; the 2021 Russian test about 1,500. OneWeb and, on public descriptions, IRIS²'s LEO layer sit in the long-lived band.
- The syndrome is not an event but a regime, and it arrives as attrition: rising conjunction rates, more manoeuvring, less margin, then losses, operators raising or abandoning shells, insurers withdrawing. The timescale is months to years, not minutes. Nothing about it is regional: one actor's fragments degrade every operator's shell.

The point for this study is structural. The scenario the connectivity layer exists for, a deliberate or accidental loss of terrestrial and satellite service over a region during a crisis, is also the scenario in which low orbit is most likely to be degraded on purpose. A resilience layer should not share the failure mode of the thing it backs up. A satellite constellation, European or otherwise, does. A stratospheric layer has no cascade mode: a lost balloon falls out of the sky within hours, nothing accumulates, and a fleet lost in a conflict is rebuilt by launch in days from a national site. That argument does not need the balloon to be cheaper than the satellite. It needs the balloon to work, which is what the pilot tests.

**What the stratosphere does not give.** It is not free of competition either: aviation, military airspace, spectrum interference, weather-limited operating volumes, launch corridors, recovery areas and cross-border drift all constrain it, and a balloon's radio footprint crosses borders whether or not the vehicle does. What the host state controls is the authorisation over its own territory, which no other operator can file for or crowd. That is the sovereignty the layer offers, and it is the reason the layer is regional.

## What a buyer asks first

A civil-protection procurement officer asks for a service specification before a platform roadmap: activation time, area, concurrent users, uplink and downlink per user, indoor-loss percentile, availability in rain, maximum incident duration, simultaneous-event assumption, cyber assurance, who is the operator of record, who pays when a 50 kg object misses the drop zone, and why the sovereign pilot flies on a US vehicle. A HAPS operator asks for the mass, power, thermal and recovery budgets and the weather minima at the launch site. A space-policy analyst asks why a 2032 European vehicle is being sold against 2026 Starlink and 2029 IRIS². This note answers the last question and the first part of the first; the specification itself is the next document.

## Approvals: the delays, and what settles each one

The register's slowest item is permissions, not hardware: W46 puts hangar consenting at five to ten years under ordinary planning law. Every row below is administrative. None of them waits on a technology; each is a signature, and the table names whose. The technical gates in the upgrade-path table (night power, uplink closure, dwell, recovery) are the only delays a pen cannot shorten. The political environment of 2025–26 has produced instruments that shorten some of the administrative rows, and the shortening is specific. The table says, for each thing that has to be approved, how long it takes today, what could cut it, who has to decide, and where that instrument stood on 17 October 2026. Times are estimates.

Three conclusions follow. First, the schedule for everything in this table is set by signatures, so the right form for the pilot is one instrument a government signs once: a pilot decree that designates the launch sites under the fast track, grants the standing airspace arrangement and termination rules, directs the regulator's temporary spectrum authorisation, indemnifies the operator, and instructs the civil-protection agency to contract the host operator for roaming and 112. Seven of the eight rows are pens of state; the eighth, the operator agreement, is a contract that the decree can require. Second, the political tailwind collapses one row, the sites, from five to ten years to about five months, and only for a state willing to call a balloon launch site a defence readiness project. That designation is the single signature that changes the schedule most, and it carries a trade: the civil layer rides a defence instrument, which is the framing this study has kept it out of. It can be done honestly as dual-use civil-protection infrastructure, and the note that proposes it should say so in those words. Third, nothing in the new instruments touches the rows that are national and bilateral: airspace, spectrum, operator agreements and neighbours' consent. Those are settled one state at a time, by a decree, a licence and a contract, and the pilot's host state is where all four are settled first. Sixty fields can be built fast under the new law; sixty fields can fly only where those four are signed.

## What stays off the table at every phase

Autonomous 40 kg gliders at hundreds of descents a day; 2 Gbps on a 40 kg payload; hemispheric loops, the Canada loop and transit of any state's airspace without its authorisation; a fuel cell on a helium vehicle, or burning lift gas as the primary night power on the light vehicle; cameras, jammer geolocation and ship tracking; any claim of service in contested airspace; and "95 % coverage" until it is computed from tracks.

## What could stop phase 1

- No state agrees to host a winter campaign with a standing authorisation and a drop zone. Approach two candidates in parallel this quarter.
- Aerostar has no slots, or prices the service beyond the budget. Ask now, ask World View too, and run the tower and aerostat tests regardless.
- The host operator will not lend 10–20 MHz under a test licence or sign the roaming and 112 arrangements. Open a second operator and a regulator conversation before contracts.
- Insurance for a 40 kg object over populated country cannot be placed at a sane premium. Get the quotation with the vehicle quotation.
- The weather window closes: a January campaign at 55°N needs launch days with low surface wind and gets few. Plan a six-week window and accept slips.
- The US-origin vehicle becomes a political objection before the pilot flies. The answer is that it is a pilot, the data belong to the host state, and the European vehicle procurement starts in phase 2.
