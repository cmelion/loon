# Cost, funding and the Starlink comparison

*Research note, 2026-10-17, revised the same day after a five-model external review (see `medium/panel_review.md` for the method; this round's record is kept outside the repo). Hand-written from `cost_model.py` / `results/cost_comparison.md`, `combined_cost.py`, `results/cost_reduction.md`, `results/outage_scenario.csv` and `TECHNICAL_ISSUES.md` §1, plus the 2026 outside figures listed at the end. Not generated by a script. Outside numbers are marked (secondary) or (estimate). Exchange rate €0.87 per US dollar.*

## 0. What this note can and cannot settle

The repository prices a fleet. It does not yet price the thing a government would be buying, which is a defined service (who is covered, at what rate, how many at once, within how many hours, for how long) measured against the cheapest alternative that delivers the same thing. Until that service and that counterfactual are written down, every ratio below is context, not a verdict. The review made that point five times over, and it is right.

Two numbers should travel together everywhere: the area cost, where the layer is 12–53× Starlink depending on the case, and the per-person cost, where the planning case is about €10–14 a year. The first describes footprint geometry and will be quoted against the article; the second describes the public bill. Neither replaces the other.

## 1. What the layer costs

Region: 10.9 M km² (the `analyze.py` box, 36–62°N, including sea). Population inside it is not yet computed from a raster; the EU, UK, Ukraine and Turkey sum to roughly 640 M before the latitude trim, so a band of 550–650 M is honest. For a public-bill figure the cleaner denominator is the EU-27's 450 M, since that is who would pay under an EU instrument.

For scale, IRIS² is now put at €15.7 B over 12 years, about two-thirds public (secondary, September 2026, not checked against a primary document). That is €1.31 B a year in total, €0.87 B of it public: €2.90 and €1.90 per EU resident respectively. The planning-case layer is 4.8× IRIS²'s total annual spend and 7.2× its public spend. Starlink's Connectivity segment spent somewhere near €7 B in 2025 by a rough cash proxy (revenue $11.4 B less EBITDA $7.2 B, plus about $4.2 B of capex that analysts infer rather than the filing states; secondary, low confidence). That figure covers the whole planet and is quoted here only for scale.

The review was unanimous that "autonomy is worth more than any price tag" cannot be the premise of a budget case. A premium is the extra cost of a defined benefit over a defined alternative. The alternative here is not Starlink broadband; it is the cheapest combination of allied-controlled satellite capacity (IRIS², OneWeb), pre-positioned terminals and a direct-to-cell contract that delivers the same emergency service. Nobody has priced that bundle. Until someone does, the per-person figures above are the gross cost of the layer, not its premium.

The review rejected the household-dish argument I made in the first draft, and it was right to: putting a Starlink dish in every household (250 M × €350 ≈ €87 B) is the cost of giving everyone dish broadband, not the cost of matching phone-direct service, because Starlink's own direct-to-cell product also reaches ordinary phones. Dishes belong in the comparison with broadband, and there they show that a few hundred thousand pre-positioned institutional terminals (one per thousand people, about €0.2 B, €30–40 M a year) are a cheap and mature alternative for hospitals, stations and vehicles. What the layer offers individuals over DTC is rate and control, not the absence of a terminal.

## 4. How to pay for it

There are two products, and the article prices one while arguing for the other.

**A permanent coverage layer, €6.3 B a year in the planning case.** The only funding shape that fits a number this size is the IRIS² one: a dedicated legal basis, a line in the 2028–2034 EU budget, member-state and third-country contributions, and a concessionaire. A concessionaire will want availability payments and demand guarantees, because unlike IRIS² there is no governmental-satcom or commercial-broadband revenue underneath. Offsets exist on paper, mobile-operator wholesale for rural and emergency cover, meteorological data from the flights, tracking receivers, a Canadian share, but none is contracted and the review's advice is to budget them at zero until one is. Spectrum is the largest not yet estimated input: if operators supply it in kind, they hold the product.

**A readiness capability, which I now put at €0.2–0.5 B a year (estimate, revised up).** You pay to be able to surge rather than for coverage that stands there. The first draft said €0.1–0.2 B; the review pointed out that the repo's own fixed lines already span €30–180 M (10–15 sites at €1.5–6 M, an operations centre at €10–50 M, regulatory standing at €5–40 M) before anything is stocked, exercised or kept in production. Sized for one 300 km winter voice-and-text surge lasting 30 days (`outage_scenario.csv`: 254 balloons aloft, 443 launches a day, €5.5 M a day, €165 M in all), the stock is about 13,300 envelopes and roughly 500 radios with turnaround spares: €14 M of envelopes at the redesign price, €130 M at today's, and €12 M of radios. Add two exercises a year that actually fly, a retained envelope line, recovery crews and vehicles, insurance, and the development bill for the three unproven items, and €0.2–0.5 B is the range; it could still be low if the production base has to be kept warm at surge scale. Winter degraded-data service (848 aloft, 1,476 launches a day) needs about 31 launch sites near the zone at the model's 48 launches a site, which a readiness fleet of 10–15 sites cannot do; that mission belongs to the permanent layer or not at all.

On instruments: the first draft said this fits rescEU, the European Defence Fund and NATO resilience money. The review's correction stands. rescEU could host an emergency-communications capacity only after an eligibility decision and a procurement structure; the EDF funds collaborative defence research and development, not the running of a civil fleet; NATO resilience commitments are national obligations, not a budget. The credible first route is national civil-protection and defence budgets for a pilot, with research money for the radio, envelope and vehicle, and an EU decision later if the pilot earns one.

Which to argue: readiness is the more credible policy case, but its standing cost is a desk estimate outside the model, and it inherits the 2 Gbps radio from the outage table just as the permanent layer does. The permanent layer is defensible per person only once the service, the counterfactual and the development bill are written down.

## 5. What the layer could do that Starlink cannot, with the qualification on each

1. **Serve ordinary phones at useful rates.** About 29 dB less path loss than from 550 km straight overhead, closer to 20 dB at the edge of a 50 km footprint. Starlink's own direct-to-cell service and AST already reach phones at low rates, so the differentiator is rate and control, and the rate depends on the 2 Gbps radio. Spread over the 600 M people invoked in the funding argument, 7.7 Tbps is about 13 kbps each at full capacity; the service has to be stated with a concurrency figure.
2. **Put capacity where it is needed.** Balloons can be concentrated over a 300 km zone to 1.7 Tbps of aggregate payload capacity, about three times Starlink's share there, because a constellation can steer beams but cannot add satellites quickly. The 1.7 Tbps assumes 848 independent 2 Gbps cells with enough spectrum reuse and 85 edge trunks of backhaul; none of that is dimensioned. The price is 472–1,476 launches a day and five to thirty-six hours to first coverage.
3. **Keep a cut-off region talking to itself.** A local core on the balloons could connect phones inside an island when the ground network is gone. Starlink's laser links can also bypass a failed local gateway, so the claim is narrower than it sounds, and the core-network work (authentication, 112 routing and location, timing without GNSS) is not priced.
4. **Reduce dependence on one foreign operator.** European ownership removes the single off-switch the series is about. It does not remove dependence on foreign chips and software, spectrum partners, launch and recovery permissions from several states, gateways, or air defence; at 20 km a balloon is a slow target wherever the airspace is contested.
5. **Bring the hardware home.** Payloads return every flight, which allows inspection and payload changes by mission. It is also a recovery dependence and a recurring cost, and it fails where landing sites are inaccessible or hostile.
6. **Need no launch vehicle.** Ground launch from European sites with European hydrogen; the only option in `cost_comparison.md` with no launch dependency. It buys resilience, not cost: launch-price shocks move the satellite figures by less than 2×.
7. **Jamming geometry, in some cases.** Around 20 dB against a distant ground jammer on the uplink, and a steep arrival angle that a directional terminal can use. These are distance terms rather than margins, the uplink into a balloon has the opposite geometry, ordinary phones cannot use the panel, and nearby or airborne jammers defeat both systems.

Latency is dropped from the list: propagation to 20 km is sub-millisecond, but end-to-end latency is set by processing and backhaul, and for voice and text it does not matter.

## 6. Where it falls short

1. Cost per square kilometre: 12–53× depending on the case; no stack of engineering levers keeps 95 % year-round service within 5× of Starlink (`cost_reduction.md`).
2. Capacity: 77 Gbps with Loon-class radios, 7.7 Tbps only if the 2 Gbps radio exists, against about 21 Tbps attributable to the region. The backhaul line in the model (€156 a flight) is an allowance, not a dimensioned network.
3. Winter: flights of 1.3–1.9 days from January to March, three to four times the summer launch rate. Winter is where the money goes.
4. Coverage is a footprint count, not an availability. Drifting nodes cluster; population-weighted availability, outage duration and winter tails have not been computed.
5. Time to coverage: five to thirty-six hours, against a constellation already overhead.
6. Weather: launches stop in the storm the layer exists to cover. Satellites stay in orbit, although Starlink has its own rain, wind, power and gateway failure modes.
7. Airspace and safety: around 800 glider descents a day through controlled airspace, landings at 15–20 m/s, overflight permissions from some thirty states, and air-traffic rules for lost links and landing zones that go well beyond permission to fly.
8. Spectrum: a balloon on one operator's band cannot lawfully keep transmitting when the wind carries it into the next country without an emergency-roaming framework that does not exist.
9. Environment: 5,000 t a year of polyethylene with the redesigned envelope, 27,500 t with today's, a third of it landing at sea, and 6,400 t of hydrogen a year in the baseline. The review expects this, not cost, to dominate public reaction.
10. Reach: no sustained service or recovery over oceans or in the Arctic; the transatlantic loop is transit, not coverage.
11. Maturity and schedule: radio, envelope and vehicle unproven, one year of winds, a private repository, and no development bill or date for a first operational year, while IRIS² and direct-to-cell will be in service before a pilot could close those gaps.
12. Recovery in a crisis: the 98 % return is a peacetime result; roads, ports, GNSS and safe landing areas may not be available when the layer is actually needed.

## 7. A paragraph for the article

Per square kilometre a satellite constellation is one to two orders of magnitude cheaper, and nothing in this work changes that. Per person, the planning case is about €14 a year for every EU resident, roughly five times what IRIS² costs in total and seven times what it costs the public. What that buys is a phone-direct layer under European control that can concentrate capacity over one region and needs no launch vehicle. Whether it is worth the premium depends on three things that have not been shown, a 2 Gbps radio, a thin envelope that holds pressure for weeks, and a lighter vehicle flown in the model, and on a comparison nobody has yet made against the cheapest allied alternative for the same emergency service. A readiness capability, at well under a euro a head, is the more credible first purchase, and even that is a desk estimate until its standing cost is modelled.
