## 2. The blackout section, replaced (from "The clearest test is a regional blackout" to the end of the Starlink paragraph)

> The clearest test is a regional blackout. We modelled a zone 300 km in radius losing towers, fibre and grid power (`outage_scenario.py`): about 34 million people at 120 per km², roughly France's average density. Demand assumes 15 % of people active at 100 kbps for voice and text, or 5 % at 1 Mbps for degraded data. Drift speeds come from the 2025 runs.
>
> Capacity per balloon sets the fleet, and it is the figure we have revised since the first sizing. That sizing assumed 2 Gbps a balloon. The radio study in the repository (`results/radio_and_backhaul.md`) finds 2 Gbps plausible as a peak outdoor downlink on the 90–100 kg payload the simulator flew, with a 1.6–2 m array and about a kilowatt through the night, and about 0.5–1.5 Gbps in the busy hour; on the 40 kg payload the cost case prices it is out of reach, and the realistic figure is tens to a few hundred megabits. We found no published measured aggregate above about 100 Mbps from any stratospheric platform; Loon delivered tens of megabits. The table below is the first sizing scaled by capacity, which is valid in this model because the fleet is set by demand divided by capacity well above the 56-balloon geometric floor; it does not add spectrum reuse, interference or launch-site limits, which a fleet in the thousands would meet.
>
> For a 300 km zone (demand 0.5 Tbps for voice and text, 1.7 Tbps for degraded data), voice and text, at €12,400 a flight:
>
> - **Heavy payload at 2 Gbps peak, the first sizing:** 254 balloons aloft; 141 launches a day in summer, 443 in winter; about €1.8 M and €5.5 M a day.
> - **Heavy payload at the busy hour, 1 Gbps and 0.5 Gbps:** 509 and 1,018 balloons; 283 and 566 launches a day in summer, 886 and 1,772 in winter; €3.5 M and €7 M a day in summer, €11 M and €22 M in winter.
> - **The 40 kg vehicle in the estimate, at 0.2 Gbps:** 2,540 balloons; 1,410 launches a day in summer, 4,430 in winter; about €17.5 M and €55 M a day. We do not regard that as a service anyone would buy. A smaller service restricted to responder traffic and messaging would need its own demand sizing, which this table does not give.
>
> First balloon arrival is 14–36 h in summer and 5–11 h in winter at every capacity, since drift sets it; usable capacity follows at the launch rate above. Degraded data at 1 Mbps for 5 % of the zone needs about 3.4 times the balloons at any capacity and is not reachable below the heavy payload. The heavier-payload rows reuse the light vehicle's flight price, so they are capacity sensitivities, not service estimates. At the €11,100 a flight of Finding 5, every cost here is about 10 % lower.
>
> Starlink's area share of its capacity over the same zone is about 0.55 Tbps, a rough benchmark rather than deliverable local capacity, and every user needs a dish and power. Traffic leaves the zone over laser links between balloons; the backhaul study plans on 500–700 km for a link that holds, not the 1,000 km of the geometric horizon at 20 km. It reaches working networks outside the zone through gateways in the ring where grid and fibre are still up, or allied satellites.

## 5. "What is not solved yet": the first two bullets, replaced

> - **Winter winds.** January–March flights last 1.3–1.9 days; winter needs three to four times the summer launch rate. Mitigation: treat winter as a transit product, balloons crossing on the conveyor with radios recovered to drop zones, and size the first service to what that allows. Hemispheric loops and the Canada loop are out of the operational case (`results/mitigation_research.md`).
> - **Capacity per balloon.** 2 Gbps is an assumption, not a test, and it needs the 90–100 kg payload; on the 40 kg vehicle the cost case prices, expect tens to a few hundred megabits (`results/radio_and_backhaul.md`). Mitigation: one winter flight with a commercial base station, measured to phones on the ground.

## 6. Finding 7: the Canada result stands; the loop beyond Europe does not

Finding 7 ends "On balance Canada helps scalability." The measured gain stands: launching from western Canada nearly doubled useful time per flight on the 36 flights flown. What the mitigation review withdrew (W6 and W20 in `results/mitigation_research.md`) is the loop beyond Europe, which drifts to 79–83°N into polar night and clips Chinese airspace on nearly every flight, and it noted that the Canada flights ran 60 days with a fuel cell and a propeller the vehicle does not have (W31: 36 flights). Replace the last sentence of Finding 7 with:

> On the 36 flights flown, Canada doubles each balloon's useful time, on a simulated vehicle with a fuel cell and propulsion, and only if every flight is terminated over Europe: the loop beyond, into polar night and Chinese airspace, is out of the operational case (`results/mitigation_research.md`, W6 and W20). The Canada conveyor stays a measured result to be re-flown on the light vehicle, not a line in the cost case.
